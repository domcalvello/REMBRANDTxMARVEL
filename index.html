Rembrandt van Rijn
Self-Portrait, 1650

A collection sealed from history.

In 2020, a group of paintings attributed to Rembrandt van Rijn was uncovered inside a wall in the attic of a private European residence.

The works appear to record the passage of American mutants through Europe in 1650. Their frames are attributed to Herman Doomer, the celebrated Amsterdam woodworker whose craft gave each sitter a distinct architectural presence.

What follows is an imagined exhibition of that lost encounter: strange abilities rendered with sober light, human gravity, and the patient scrutiny of portraiture.

Recovered from a wall. Rebuilt in public.

From the first torchlit glimpse to the final gallery placement, the collection passed through a chain of conservators, frame specialists, and scholars.

The paintings

Portraiture gives the impossible a human scale. Insignia recede; posture, gesture, cloth, metal, and light become evidence.

Portrait of Charles Xavier, Scholar

Charles Francis Xavier

Xavier is depicted without insignia or weapon, seated formally against a restrained field of warm light. The composition places intellectual authority above physical spectacle, presenting the leader of the travelers as scholar, teacher and statesman.

“His body keeps its chair, yet the room itself seems to belong to his mind.”

Imagined studio note

Portrait of Henry McCoy, Called the Beast

Henry McCoy

McCoy appears among books by candlelight, his formidable form deliberately contrasted with the habits of a scholar. Rembrandt’s light settles most strongly across the sitter’s brow and hands, emphasizing thought over physical strength.

“They call him beast, though I have met few men so governed by reason.”

Imagined studio note

Portrait of Ororo Munroe

Ororo Munroe

Munroe is shown frontally in pale ceremonial dress, her white hair and clouded eyes emerging dramatically from the dark field behind her. The elaborate gilded frame reinforces a portrait more reminiscent of nobility than military service.

“Her stillness carries the warning of a sky before thunder.”

Imagined studio note

Portrait of Piotr Rasputin, the Man of Steel

Piotr Nikolaievitch Rasputin

Rasputin’s metallic body is rendered with the discipline normally reserved for polished armor. Yet his relaxed posture and elaborate collar turn the spectacle of his transformation into a formal portrait of dignity and restraint.

“Iron has the greater weight, but gentleness is the harder thing to paint.”

Imagined studio note

Portrait of Anna Marie, Called Rogue

Anna Marie

Anna Marie stands in restrained green and ochre garments, the gloves becoming the portrait’s most consequential detail. What first appears to be ordinary dress gradually reveals itself as protection—for both sitter and observer.

“She keeps her hands covered not for fear of the world, but from fear of what her touch may take from it.”

Two portraits of Logan

One sitter, divided by what the world sees first.

Portrait of Logan, Called Wolverine

Logan

Logan is portrayed in yellow and dark blue with his claws deliberately exposed, an unusual decision in an otherwise conventional seated portrait. Rembrandt places the weapons directly in the light while allowing much of the sitter’s body to disappear into shadow.

“He carries the manner of a man who expects every room eventually to become a battlefield.”

Imagined studio note

Portrait of Logan

Logan

Painted without weapons or recognizable uniform, the second portrait strips Logan of the identity presented in the preceding work. His expression becomes the subject: guarded, exhausted and intensely human.

“In the first portrait I painted what men fear. In the second, I painted the man who must live with it.”

Imagined Rembrandt studio note

Portrait of Scott Summers, Captain

Scott Summers

Summers appears armored and cloaked, his eyes concealed behind an unfamiliar mechanical visor emitting a narrow crimson light. His raised hand suggests adjustment rather than aggression, presenting the device as restraint rather than weapon.

“The curious instrument across his eyes is not made so that he may see, but so that others may safely look upon him.”

Imagined studio note

Portrait of Kurt Wagner, Called Nightcrawler

Kurt Wagner

Wagner is painted with none of the theatrical alarm his appearance might have invited from a seventeenth-century observer. His direct gaze and composed stance make the extraordinary physical traits almost secondary to the sitter himself.

“The eye first sees a devil. Remain with him a little longer and the error belongs to the eye.”

Imagined studio note

Portrait of Remy LeBeau, Called Gambit

Remy LeBeau

LeBeau holds a single playing card between gloved fingers as violet light gathers around its edge. Rather than explain the phenomenon, the portrait preserves the moment immediately before its purpose becomes apparent.

“He handles cards as another man handles a pistol: lightly, confidently, and never without intention.”

Imagined studio note

Portrait of Eric Lensherr, Called Magneto

Eric Lensherr

Lensherr is shown standing in crimson armor, one hand raised as though addressing an unseen audience. His posture is neither defensive nor openly hostile; the painting instead presents a man absolutely convinced of his own authority.

“Some men enter a room and ask to be heard. He enters already certain that history is listening.”

Imagined studio note

Portrait of Cain Marko, Called Juggernaut

Cain Marko

Marko fills nearly the entire painted field, his enormous helmet and deep crimson armor compressing the space around him. Unlike the more elaborate portraits preceding it, the work relies upon mass, symmetry and direct confrontation.

“There was little room left upon the canvas once the man had entered it.”

Imagined studio note
